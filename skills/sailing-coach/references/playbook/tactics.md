# Tactics

Racing the boats around you: lanes, crossings, leverage, fleet position. Our data has no other boats tracked, so most of this is for the question at the end of the debrief and for reading patterns like "many tacks with no shift behind them".

## Know who you're racing
- Track the boats ahead, behind and even with you, across the whole course, not just the nearest ones. A "ladder rung" (a line square to the wind through your boat) shows who's actually ahead. [T1, T2]
- Make predictions about the wind and check them as the beat unfolds; keep a running story of what's happening. [T2]

## Lanes
- A clear lane is worth more than a small angle advantage: nearby boats slow you, limit options and bring rules trouble. [T1]
- A starboard boat a few lengths to leeward and slightly ahead is a "blocker": port boats have to tack away or duck you both. [T1]
- To hold a lane, sail a touch higher and accept being a little slower; you'll gain later from being free to go where you want. [SWU]
- **Data:** a high `no_shift` tack count, especially in a race with far more tacks than the others, often means tacks for clear air or to escape a bad lane. Ask about it in the closing question rather than calling it a strategy error.

## Crossings
- Duck when you like the lane you're in, even on starboard; accelerate through the duck so you can't be covered. [T1]
- To tack instead: lee-bow if you can cross comfortably enough to set up in their bad air; otherwise tack early and give yourself options without forcing the issue. [T1]
- "Cross them when you can, don't let them cross you": when a shift puts you ahead, take it across their bow to bank the gain; when you're losing, stay away until it shifts back. [SWR]

## Leverage and consolidation
- Gains and losses from a shift scale with how far apart you are across the wind. Ahead: stay close and between the fleet and the mark. Behind: more separation gives more chance to gain, and more risk. [T2, W2]
- In an oscillating breeze keep leverage small; in a persistent shift, leverage toward the shift pays. [W2]
- "Foot in a lift, pinch in a header": footing on a lift builds separation toward the next shift; pinching through an unavoidable header limits the leverage of boats to windward. [T2]
- Later in the beat, lead the pack back toward the middle on the long tack; that keeps you off the laylines. When in doubt, protect the right (starboard advantage near the mark). [T1]
- Approaching a pack from the side: if they're already in the next shift or pressure, tack and lead them back; if not, consider crossing and edging out to what they missed. Past ~80% of the way to the layline, lead back. [T2]

## Covering
- Loose cover when ahead of a pack: keep them between you and the mark without hurting their air. Tight cover only to force one boat somewhere or slow it. [T1]

## Fleet-level lessons from Etchells championships
- In big, tight fleets, consistency beats risk: many winning teams start in the middle third and work to the favored side up the beat, keeping off the edges after a bad result. [N2, N3, N4]
- Teams that won kept roles clear: one trimmer owns the upwind mode (high vs. fast), the kite trimmer calls downwind height, the tactician calls "my boat" for short tactical moves like a lee-bow. [N3]
