# Upwind strategy

Where to go on the beat: shifts, pressure, current, laylines. Tactics against other boats are in `tactics.md`.

## Reading the pattern first
The single biggest strategic call is whether the wind is oscillating (swinging around a steady average) or persistent (trending one way). The right plays are opposite, so decide before the start and keep checking. [W1, SW1]
- **Oscillating is more likely when:** offshore wind, patchy/puffy water, post-front gradient breeze, boats lifted and headed on both sides, gains on both sides, tack headings swinging within a range, the favored end of the line flipping back and forth. [W1]
- **Persistent is more likely when:** onshore wind, steady water or clearly more pressure on one side, thermal sea breeze, the further out one side the more boats are lifted/headed, one side always gains, tack headings trending one way, one end of the line getting steadily more favored, the RC moving the windward mark. [W1]
- Unsure? Oscillating is the more common pattern; stay nearer the middle and keep looking. If you can see where the next shift comes from, sail toward it; that works in both patterns. [W1]
- Allow enough observation time: a long oscillation can pass for a persistent shift. Even on a "trending right" day, a 30-minute beat can be steady or oscillate. [W2]
- **Data:** the per-beat wind trend from our tacking headings (`shifts` table) and the side we worked. A beat that trended 5°+ one way while we were 70%+ on the other side is the classic miss. A sea-breeze venue leans persistent by the list above, but our own July data had beats trending both ways, so check each day rather than assume.

## Oscillating shifts
- Tack on the headers and stay on the lifted tack; that's the shortest path to the mark. Record high/low headings on each tack before the start and use the median as the reference: 5–10° below it on your tack means headed. [SW1, SZ1]
- Get onto the lifted tack soon after the start and try to be first to the next shift; in an oscillating breeze that matters more than winning the favored end. [SZ1]
- Don't tack at the first sign of a header; sail a little into it so you don't sail straight out of the shift on the other tack, and so you don't tack on a lull (a velocity header). [SZ1]
- If you can't tell lift from header, sail the tack that points closer to the mark (the long tack): it's more likely the lifted one and it keeps you in the middle. [SW1]
- No compass? Watch the boats around you: sail toward where you're gaining, cross boats when a shift lets you, and don't let boats cross you (tack below them and wait for the next shift). [SW1]
- Near the top mark, treat the last oscillation like a persistent shift: if a cycle takes ~4 min and you're within ~4 min of the mark, there's only one shift left to play. [SW1]
- **Data:** tack calls (`header` / `lift` / `no_shift`) and the "sailed headed ≥45 s" count. More lift tacks than header tacks means the compass isn't being used. Many `no_shift` tacks are a lane or habit question (see `tactics.md`).
- **Cue:** trimmer or tactician calls the compass against the beat's median every 30 s; tack when 5° headed and the boat is at speed.

## Persistent shifts
- Sail toward the shift, and sail into the header rather than tacking on it: the boat closest to the new wind gains the most, in proportion to its lateral separation (leverage). Taking sterns early is often the price. [SW1, W2]
- Only commit as far as your confidence: if you're 50/50 on a veer, don't bang the corner. Weigh what you'd give up to get there (unfavored end, a few minutes of bad air) against how big you expect the shift to be. [W2]
- Sail fast (bow down a touch) toward the shift and defend the lane in that direction. [W2]
- Geographic shifts near shore, and current differences across the course, behave like persistent shifts: they grow as you sail into them. [W2]
- Don't overstand: the shift usually keeps coming after you tack for the mark. [SW1, W2]
- Think ahead to the next leg: a big persistent shift changes the run (see `downwind.md`). [W2]

## When the lifted tack is wrong
- To reach a persistent or final shift, sail the tack that gets you there first even if it's headed. [SZ1]
- In light air, or when one area clearly has more pressure, get to the pressure without delay. [SZ1]
- Near the end of the beat, avoid getting to the laylines early even on a lift. [SZ1]

## Laylines
- The laylines are a dead end in shifting wind: a lift makes you overstand; a header lets the boats inside tack and cross. Any change in wind costs you once you're on one. [SW1, SZ2]
- Don't plan to reach the layline until about 10–15 lengths from the mark, closer in shifty or puffy wind. Unless you're in the top few boats, never join the layline more than ~10 lengths out: you'll sail in the bad air of the parade. [SZ3, N2]
- Before that, find a clear lane a few lengths short of the layline and tack onto it near the three-length zone. From the left, a good play is tacking to port ~4 lengths short of the port layline. [SZ3, SZ4]
- Far from the mark, tack away from the layline before you're ~80% of the way to it. Halfway up the beat, if you're twice as close to one layline as the other, check the strategy; three times as close needs a very good reason. [SZ4, SZ2]
- Calling it: tacking angle (headings on each tack) is the best reference. In an Etchells the layline is when the mark is roughly abeam to slightly forward of abeam. Use two methods (eyeball + compass bearing from the opposite tack's heading) and check against boats on the other tack. Waves and lulls widen the tacking angle; more breeze narrows it. [W3, SZ2]
- Overstanding slightly is usually safer than understanding slightly (two extra tacks, pinching, being tacked on). Current pushing you down, adverse current, more breeze past the layline, or a port approach through a starboard queue are reasons to go a little farther. Don't make it a habit. [W4]
- Make the final tack a good one: not slow, not in a wave, and don't overshoot the turn. [SZ2]
- **Data:** our overstand angle (includes leeway), the layline tack call, extra distance vs. ideal on the beat, and whether the first windward mark was fetched from far out. Overstanding by 7–11° a few times a weekend is the common pattern.

## Current
- Current moves the laylines. Pushed toward the layline → biggest risk is overstanding; approach below the normal layline. Pushed away (adverse) → easy to underlay and pinch; overstand a little. [SW2]
- Current is stronger in deeper water and around points and harbor mouths; it turns along the shore first. Choppier water often means current against the wind; smoother means with it. Check it at a fixed mark before the start. [SW2]
- There's no lee-bow effect from pinching into current, and a uniform current doesn't favor either tack. [SW2]
- **Data:** COG vs. heading difference beyond normal leeway (~5–6° for the Etchells) on one tack only hints at current. A port/starboard speed-over-ground gap of more than a few tenths of a knot on the same beat is another hint (our own rule of thumb, not from the sources). Our speeds are SOG, so say so when it matters.
