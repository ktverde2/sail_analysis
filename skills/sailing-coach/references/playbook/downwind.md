# Downwind

Runs are where a fast boat can lose what it gained upwind. Order of priority in light to medium air: pressure, clear air, then shifts. In breeze, waves and control come first.

## Pressure first
- Puffs matter far more downwind than upwind: they hit harder and you stay in them much longer because you're travelling with them. Get to the first puff after the mark and stay in pressure. [D1, D2]
- Someone looks aft the whole run to call the next puff and where it's going. When a puff hits, square the pole, ease the main, bear away with it (gybe if needed) to stay in it longer. [SW1, D1]
- If one side clearly has more breeze, get there first. A persistent shift that also brings more pressure trumps the "sail away from the shift" rule below. [D2, SW1]

## Angles
- "Up in the lulls, down in the puffs", but only bear away after the boat has accelerated, with pressure in the sheets. Head back up as soon as speed or pressure fades. [D1, D2]
- Expecting the wind to fade later in the leg? Sail low early while there's breeze, and save the higher, faster angles for the light end. The reverse if it'll build. Sailing high early toward pressure that then fades is a common way to get passed. [SW1, D3]
- In heavy air, point at the mark and focus on control, waves and tactics. [D1]
- **Data:** run TWA (heading-based) and downwind VMG in steady segments; the `downwind` chart of SOG vs. angle. Extra distance vs. the straight line on runs shows how much the angles cost or paid.

## Shifts
- Gybe on the lifts and stay on the headed gybe; it points you closer to the mark at the same speed. It's the mirror image of tacking on headers. [SW1, D4]
- Sail away from a persistent shift on the first gybe so both gybes end up relatively headed. If you'll only see one or two shifts on the run, treat them as persistent. [SW1, D4]
- Shifts are harder to feel downwind because the boat keeps moving. Use shroud telltales or the masthead fly for direction, and their droop to tell a shift from a change in pressure. [D4]
- Playing shifts pays most in light to medium, steady-pressure air. In puffy air the puffs matter more; in very light air, find the wind first. [D4]
- Absent other reasons, don't gybe early; stay on the long gybe and keep off the downwind laylines, just as upwind. Every gybe costs distance. [D2, SW1]

## Clear air and other boats
- A boat 5–10 lengths behind can take your air; check by sighting along the telltales or fly toward boats astern. Luff or bear away to clear, or gybe away if needed, and make sure a gybe still leaves you clear if they follow. [D1, D2]
- Don't let a boat roll over you to windward: head up early to show you'll defend. [D3]
- To attack, use your wind shadow on the boat ahead. [D2]

## Etchells technique
- Get the mast raked as far forward as possible downwind: lever/ram released, all blocks out in front of the mast, backstay (and backstay fine tune) fully off, jib halyard tensioned to the jib tack to pull the rig forward. In light air, aim for a slight inversion in the middle of the mast. Most of the fleet doesn't get the mast far enough forward. [N1, N2, D5]
- Pole: inboard end about 30" above the gooseneck band; outboard end a little low under 8 kt, level at 8–13 kt, raised in breeze. [N1]
- Crew weight forward in light air, until the bow starts burying into the wave ahead; keep weight low in chop. [N2]
- In waves, work the sheets and body weight to catch waves, but in sync with them: one Worlds-winning team found they'd been too aggressive and gained by coordinating helm and kite trimmer to push hard only when a wave was there. [N2, N3]
- VMG-style running spinnakers can let you sail deeper with the pole forward in the right conditions; the flat runner is typically for more breeze. [N3, D5]
- The Etchells rarely broaches but can roll to windward in a big breeze. Ease the pole forward a little and sail slightly higher so the crew can stay to windward. [N2]
- **Cue:** "Mast forward, weight forward, eyes aft": one person calls pressure every 30 s.
